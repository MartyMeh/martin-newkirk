A journey across organisations you don't control
Key Swap, 2020–2021 · Product Manager, Car Next Door
Setup
A trip was the product, and almost nobody who made it happen worked for us. Instant Key needed a full installation before any car could join, which capped supply.
What to say
  • Owner had to list and keep the car available, borrower had to turn up, installer had to fit hardware, key had to be where the car was. Any one failing, and the trip quietly didn't happen.
  • I led Key Swap, a second way to join. No installation, and owner and borrower swapped the key in person. Each trip got harder, on purpose.
  • The bet: far more owners, then move the good ones to Instant Key and take the weak ones off.
  • New onboarding, booking flow and admin tools, with product, engineering, design and ops. Owners self-onboarded, and the install team posted kits.
  • Owner signups rose sharply. At the Uber acquisition: 10,000 cars, 60% on Key Swap.
Full script

At Car Next Door a completed trip was the product, and almost none of the people who made it happen worked for us. An owner had to list the car and keep it available, a borrower had to turn up, an installer had to fit hardware, and the key had to be where the car was. If any one of those failed, the trip just didn't happen, often without anyone telling us.

The original model was Instant Key. Every car got a full in-car installation, with the key in a lockbox on the vehicle. Borrowers loved it, but it meant no car could join until a technician had been out, and that capped how fast we could grow supply.

I led Key Swap, a second way to join. The owner signed up without an installation, and the owner and borrower exchanged the key in person. We knew that made each trip harder. The bet was that it would bring on far more owners, and that we could then move the good ones to Instant Key and take the weak ones off.

It was a big build for a small company: new onboarding, a new booking flow and new admin tools, delivered by product, engineering, design and operations together. It also changed the operation. Owners onboarded themselves instead of going through sales, and the installation team moved from fitting every car to posting kits.

Owner signups rose sharply. By the time Uber acquired the business, the platform had 10,000 cars and 60% of them were on Key Swap.

Several workstreams, clear trade-offs
December 2018 · About 600 cars, not profitable
If asked which project:You don't recall the exact one, only that the growth roadmap stopped. Don't name one.
Setup
Cars being stolen, and we paid for each one. Two or three a week, close to 2% of the fleet a month. A runway problem, not an ops problem.
What to say
  • Late-night meeting with the CEO and fleet team. We paused the growth roadmap overnight. No point adding owners if we couldn't keep their cars.
  • That week: I flew to Melbourne with one teammate, bought steering wheel locks, and fitted them with our installers to the riskiest cars.
  • Next: lockbox off the car where possible, reachable only after booking. Trade-off: borrowers wanted the key on the car, and that was what was being targeted.
  • Later: stronger brackets that couldn't be cut, as theft methods changed.
  • By January, below two a month. The lesson: decide what stops before what starts, and give the first fix a deadline in days.
Full script

In December 2018 we had about 600 cars and weren't profitable, and cars started being stolen in Melbourne. When a car was stolen, we paid for it. We were losing two or three a week, which is close to 2% of the fleet a month. At that rate it was a runway problem, not an operations problem.

We had a late-night meeting with the chief executive and the fleet team, and I was part of the decision about what to stop. We paused the growth roadmap that was in flight, overnight. There was no point bringing in more owners if we couldn't keep their cars.

Then we split the response into what had to happen that week and what could follow. That week, I flew to Melbourne with one teammate, we bought steering wheel locks, and we worked with our Melbourne installers to fit them to the cars most at risk. Next, where it was possible, we moved the lockbox off the car, to somewhere a borrower could reach only after booking. That was a real trade-off. Borrowers wanted the key on the car, and the key on the car was what was being targeted. Later came stronger lockbox brackets that couldn't be cut, because the method of theft kept changing.

By January we were below two thefts a month, and growth work picked up again.

What I'd take from it into Access: decide what stops before deciding what starts, and give the first fix a deadline in days, not a project plan.

Note: If they ask which growth project was paused, say you don't recall the exact project, only that the whole roadmap stopped. Don't name one.

Hyundai, investor from 2016 · Fleet manager, then product manager
Prepare:One honest sentence on how the dealership test went, because they will ask. Don't say keyless launched fleet-wide. Don't mention Ampol.
Setup
Three parties. Hyundai head office, their franchise dealers, and us. I'd have to run whatever we agreed and build it into the product.
What to say
  • Hyundai wanted keyless entry through our app for Hyundai cars, and our platform as a long test drive for buyers.
  • Dealers liked anything that sold cars, but didn't want extra process or stolen stock.
  • My position: a keyless feature for one brand isn't a marketplace feature. It's a custom build we didn't have engineers for.
  • I separated the two asks. We'd run the dealership test on the product as it was, with no dealer-only flow. Any keyless entry had to work in any car.
  • That gave them a real test without tying our roadmap to one brand.
Full script

Hyundai invested in Car Next Door in 2016, and over the next few years we tried to turn that into practical work together. I was involved first as fleet manager and then as product manager, which made me the person who'd have to run whatever we agreed and build it into the product.

There were really three parties. Hyundai head office wanted keyless entry through our app for Hyundai cars. They also wanted people thinking about buying a Hyundai to use our platform as a long test drive. Their dealers, who are franchisees rather than Hyundai itself, liked anything that might sell cars, but didn't want extra process or stolen stock. And we needed anything we built to work across a fleet that was mostly not Hyundai.

My position was simple. A keyless feature that works for one brand isn't a marketplace feature. It's a custom build for one partner, and we didn't have the engineers for it. So I separated the two asks. We agreed to run the dealership test with them, using the product as it was, with no dealer-only flow. And any keyless entry we built had to work in any car, not just a Hyundai.

That gave Hyundai and the dealers a real test without tying our roadmap to one brand.

An operational change that persisted
Key Swap after launch · Left product in September 2021
If you told story 1:Open with "Coming back to Key Swap…" and skip the setup.
Setup
The launch was the easy part. Signups were up, but utilisation was down, owners earned less, support calls rose, and borrowers felt the friction. There was real internal doubt.
What to say
  • We kept the model and changed the operation so it didn't rely on anyone pushing it.
  • We took off owners who weren't earning, encouraged lockboxes where they cut friction, and built self-install kits. The kits made the biggest difference.
  • In strategy and ops I owned the delisting policy. It turned "take weak owners off" from an occasional clean-up into a standing rule with criteria.
  • Evidence: when Uber acquired the business the following year, 60% of 10,000 cars were on Key Swap. That was well after I'd stopped owning it.
Full script

Coming back to Key Swap. The launch was the easy part. The early numbers were uncomfortable. Signups were up, but utilisation was down, owners were earning less than they expected, support calls rose, and borrowers felt the extra effort of meeting someone to get a key. Inside the company there was real doubt about whether we'd made a mistake.

We kept the model and changed the operation around it, so it wasn't relying on me or anyone else pushing it. We tracked which owners were actually earning and took the weak ones off. We encouraged lockboxes where they removed friction without a full installation. And we built self-install kits, so a good owner could move toward Instant Key without a technician visit. The kits made the biggest difference.

In September 2021 I moved out of product into strategy and operations. One of the things I owned there was a delisting policy. It turned "take weak owners off" from a clean-up we did when someone had time into a standing rule with criteria. That's what I'd point to as the change persisting. It stopped being a project and became how the operation ran.

The evidence is the fleet mix. When Uber acquired the business the following year, 60% of 10,000 cars were on Key Swap. That's well after launch, and after I'd stopped being the product owner.

When to automate, and when to use people and process
AFCA complaints, 2023 · Strategy & Operations, Uber Carshare
Setup
Insurance cover sold with trips made us a financial product, so we had to meet AFCA's complaints requirements, and we didn't. We were under Uber scrutiny, on a short timeline.
What to say
  • The obvious answer was to build a complaints system. The real job was deciding what needed software and what needed people.
  • Built: a way to complain at every point in a transaction, recording so nothing got lost, and analytics on volumes and response times.
  • Not built: escalation to the ombudsman, response standards and judgement calls. Those became policy and frontline training.
  • Software there would have taken growth developers and been less reliable than a trained team with clear rules.
  • Access has the same question at every step. A missing result might need a lab integration, or it might need a phone call.
Full script

In 2023 we found that, because we sold insurance cover with trips, the business was offering a financial product. That meant we had to meet AFCA's complaints requirements, and we didn't. We were also now part of Uber, so the scrutiny was high and the timeline was short.

The obvious answer was to build a complaints system. The real job was working out which parts needed software and which needed people. In the product, a customer needed a way to complain at every point in a transaction, and every complaint had to be recorded so nothing could get lost. That had to be built. But the escalation path through to the ombudsman, the response standards and the judgement calls were policy and frontline training, not code. Building software for those would have taken developers we needed for growth, and it would have been less reliable than a trained team with clear rules.

So we built the capture and the reporting, wrote the policy and escalation path, and trained the frontline. We also added analytics, so we could see volumes and response times without anyone compiling them by hand.

That's the same question Access has at every step. A missing result might need an integration with the lab, or it might need someone to call them.

What you're working on now: a two-sided marketplace
Photonik marketplace, Iloilo and Cebu · Trial, 2026
Be exact:No homeowner has been matched to an installer yet, and there are no installs. Don't round 30-plus quote requests into customers.
Setup
A marketplace matching homeowners who want solar with local installers. Also works for partner network questions, or as a conversion example.
What to say
  • Homeowner side is automated end to end, and I've tested it myself. A savings model for solar and batteries, then a quote request.
  • Signup for both sides was the hard call: every question adds information and loses people. Changed it several times. Homeowner conversion went from 2% to 10%, with nothing important lost.
  • The constraint now is supply. More than 30 quote requests, none yet where our installers cover.
  • Three installers approved by hand, from their websites and reviews. A person makes that call for now. Automate the checks once I know which signals predict a good installer.
  • The shape of Access: demand first, the answer depends on a partner in the right place, and the product can only promise what the network delivers.
Full script

Use this for "What are you working on now?", for questions about the partner network, or as a second example of conversion work.

At Photonik we're trialling a marketplace in Iloilo and Cebu, in the Philippines. It matches homeowners who want solar with local installers who can supply it.

The homeowner side is built and automated end to end, and I've tested the whole flow myself. A homeowner sees a model of what solar and batteries would save them, then asks for a quote. The hardest design question was signup, for homeowners and for installers. Every question we add gets us better information and loses people. I changed both flows several times to find the balance. Homeowner conversion in the Philippines went from 2% to 10%, and we still collect everything we need to quote.

The constraint now is supply. More than 30 homeowners have asked for a quote, but none yet in the areas our installers cover. So my work at the moment is building the installer network. I've approved three installers so far. Each one I checked by hand, from their website and their customer reviews. At this stage that judgement is better done by a person than automated, and I'll automate the checks once I know which signals actually predict a good installer.

This is the shape of Access. Demand arrives first, the answer depends on a partner being in the right place, and the product can only promise what the network can deliver.

Note: Be exact: no homeowner has been matched to an installer yet, and there are no completed installs. Don't round 30-plus requests into customers. Marketplace numbers aren't on the Photonik metrics page, because the product is still in testing.
